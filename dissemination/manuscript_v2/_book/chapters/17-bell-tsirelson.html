19  Bell’s Theorem and Tsirelson’s Bound

Bell’s theorem states that no local hidden variable theory can reproduce all predictions of quantum mechanics. FTD is a local hidden variable theory. This chapter explains why both statements are true simultaneously.

19.1 17.1 The apparent contradiction

Bell’s theorem (1964): Any theory satisfying locality + measurement independence gives CHSH parameter \(S \leq 2\). [THEOREM — standard result]

Tsirelson’s bound (1980): Quantum mechanics gives \(S \leq 2\sqrt{2} \approx 2.83\) for maximally entangled states at optimal angles. [THEOREM — standard result]

Experiment (Aspect 1982, loophole-free 2015): \(S = 2.83 \pm 0.02\). Quantum prediction confirmed.

FTD: A deterministic lattice with local causality (Postulate 4). Bell’s theorem applies. \(S \leq 2\) at the substrate level. Confirmed in simulation.

The resolution: \(S \leq 2\) and \(S = 2\sqrt{2}\) describe different description levels of the same system.

19.2 17.2 The two-level structure [SELECTION]

Level Description Correlation bound Why
Substrate Lattice \((s, \mathbf{J})\) deterministic, local \(S \leq 2\) Bell’s theorem applies (hidden variables)
Emergent QM (\(\psi \in \mathcal{H}\)), statistical \(S = 2\sqrt{2}\) Tsirelson’s bound (Hilbert space)

The substrate is a local hidden variable model. Bell’s theorem correctly constrains it to \(S \leq 2\). But the emergent quantum description — constructed from the complexified flux field (Chapter 18) — is a Hilbert space theory. Tsirelson’s bound correctly gives \(S = 2\sqrt{2}\) for this description. Both bounds hold at their respective levels.

The “violation” is not the lattice breaking locality. It is the coarse-grained statistical description (QM) having a richer correlation structure than the fine-grained substrate allows. This is analogous to thermodynamic entropy increasing while microscopic dynamics are reversible.

19.3 17.3 The three-level mechanism [SELECTION]

The transition from \(S = 2\) (substrate) to \(S = 2\sqrt{2}\) (emergent) proceeds through three stages:

19.3.1 Level 1: Substrate (local, deterministic) [THEOREM]

On the lattice, a pair of particles created from a void event \(0 \to (+1) + (-1)\) carries opposite charges and anti-correlated flux vectors. A measurement along axis \(\hat{a}\) yields \(\pm 1\) via sign projection: \(A = \text{sign}(\mathbf{J}_A \cdot \hat{a})\).

The correlation function for random 3D flux vectors is: \[ E(\hat{a}, \hat{b}) = \langle A \cdot B \rangle = -\frac{2}{\pi}\theta_{ab} + 1 - \frac{2}{\pi}\theta_{ab} = -1 + \frac{2\theta_{ab}}{\pi} \tag{17.1} \]

the triangle function. CHSH: \(S \leq 2\). [THEOREM — verified in Monte Carlo]

19.3.2 Level 2: Complexification (Gauss constraint) [SELECTION]

The Gauss constraint reduces the 3D flux \(\mathbf{J} \in \mathbb{R}^3\) to a 2D complex amplitude \(\psi = J_x + iJ_y\) (Chapter 18). The correlation function changes from triangle to cosine:

\[ E(\hat{a}, \hat{b}) = -\cos\theta_{ab} \tag{17.2} \]

because the 2D projection induces a \(\pi/2\) phase shift between the two components. The per-particle correlation enhancement is \(\sqrt{2}\) (from the curvature of cosine vs. triangle).

19.3.3 Level 3: Entanglement (sLoop coupling) [SELECTION]

The void event \(0 \to (+1) + (-1)\) creates a pair with shared flux history. The Gauss constraint forces the pair’s transverse flux fields to be anti-correlated: \(\psi_B = -\overline{\psi_A}\) in the complexified description. This is the singlet state:

\[ |\psi\rangle = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\left(|+\rangle|-\rangle - |-\rangle|+\rangle\right) \tag{17.3} \]

The joint probability distribution is non-factorizable: \(P(A, B | \hat{a}, \hat{b}) \neq P(A | \hat{a}) P(B | \hat{b})\). This non-factorizability, combined with the cosine correlation, produces \(S = 2\sqrt{2}\).

19.4 17.4 The singlet-state lemma [SELECTION — derivation target]

19.5 Open derivation target

The remaining gap: prove that the void event \(0 \to (+1)_A + (-1)_B\) maps to the singlet state (17.3) in the emergent Hilbert space.

The argument: (a) the Gauss constraint forces anti-correlated transverse flux between the pair; (b) in the complexified description, anti-correlated real flux with correlated phase IS the singlet; (c) the singlet is the unique \(S = 0\), \(L = 0\) state, and the void event’s perfect charge balance forces total spin zero.

This is physically well-motivated but not yet a formal proof. Closing this lemma would upgrade the entire Bell resolution from [SELECTION] to [THEOREM].

19.6 17.5 Why the three paths were wrong

The FTD literature previously explored three substrate-level resolution paths: (1) superdeterminism, (2) detection loophole, (3) continuous-to-discrete projection. All three attempted to get \(S > 2\) from the substrate. This is impossible (Bell’s theorem is correct) and unnecessary.

\(S = 2\sqrt{2}\) is a property of the emergent quantum description, not the substrate. Once QM emerges from the lattice (Chapter 18), Tsirelson’s bound follows as a theorem of Hilbert space theory. No substrate-level mechanism is needed.

19.7 17.6 Experimental compatibility [SELECTION]

The FTD prediction for Bell tests is identical to standard QM: \(S = 2\sqrt{2}\) for maximally entangled photon pairs at CHSH-optimal angles (\(\theta = \pi/8\)). This is compatible with all loophole-free Bell experiments (Hensen et al. 2015, Giustina et al. 2015, Shalm et al. 2015).

The distinction from standard QM: in FTD, the violation has a causal explanation (the shared flux history from the void event), while in standard QM, it is accepted as a brute feature of the Hilbert space structure. The experimental predictions are identical.
