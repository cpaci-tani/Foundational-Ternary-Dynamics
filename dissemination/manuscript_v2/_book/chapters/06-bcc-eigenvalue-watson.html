8  The BCC Eigenvalue and Watson Identity

This chapter contains the central algebraic identity of FTD: the BCC lattice Green’s function at the origin equals \(G^{*2}/(2\pi)\), connecting the discrete lattice to the lemniscatic constant through a product of Gamma functions. The identity was first computed by Watson (1939) and independently rediscovered in FTD; its sublattice attribution was confirmed numerically in April 2026.

8.1 6.1 The multiplicative eigenvalue [THEOREM]

The BCC sublattice Laplacian has a normalized eigenvalue that is a product of cosines:

\[ \sigma_{\text{BCC}}(\mathbf{k}) = 1 - \cos k_1 \cos k_2 \cos k_3 \tag{6.1} \]

This is structurally distinct from the SC eigenvalue (a sum of cosines) and the FCC eigenvalue (a sum of pairwise products). The distinction is decisive: products factor across axes; sums do not.

8.2 6.2 The Green’s function at the origin [THEOREM]

The BCC lattice Green’s function at the origin on the infinite lattice \(\mathbb{Z}^3\) is:

\[ G_{\text{BCC}}(0) = \frac{1}{(2\pi)^3} \int_0^{2\pi} \!\! \int_0^{2\pi} \!\! \int_0^{2\pi} \frac{dk_1 \, dk_2 \, dk_3}{1 - \cos k_1 \cos k_2 \cos k_3} \tag{6.2} \]

where the zero modes (four points in the Brillouin zone where \(\sigma_{\text{BCC}} = 0\)) contribute integrable singularities.

8.3 6.3 The geometric series factorization [THEOREM]

The key step: expand the integrand as a geometric series:

\[ \frac{1}{1 - x} = \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} x^n \quad \text{for } |x| < 1 \tag{6.3} \]

with \(x = \cos k_1 \cos k_2 \cos k_3\). Since \(|x| \leq 1\) and equals 1 only on a set of measure zero, the series converges almost everywhere:

\[ G_{\text{BCC}}(0) = \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} \frac{1}{(2\pi)^3} \int (\cos k_1 \cos k_2 \cos k_3)^n \, d^3k \tag{6.4} \]

Because the integrand is a product, it factors across the three axes:

\[ G_{\text{BCC}}(0) = \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} \left[ \frac{1}{2\pi} \int_0^{2\pi} (\cos k)^n \, dk \right]^3 \tag{6.5} \]

The 1D integral vanishes for odd \(n\). For even \(n = 2m\):

\[ \frac{1}{2\pi} \int_0^{2\pi} (\cos k)^{2m} \, dk = \frac{1}{4^m} \binom{2m}{m} \tag{6.6} \]

Therefore:

\[ G_{\text{BCC}}(0) = \sum_{m=0}^{\infty} \left[ \frac{1}{4^m} \binom{2m}{m} \right]^3 \tag{6.7} \]

This is the cube of central binomial coefficients, a classical object in combinatorics and special function theory.

8.4 6.4 The Watson identity [THEOREM]

The sum of cubed central binomial coefficients evaluates to:

\[ \sum_{m=0}^{\infty} \left[ \frac{1}{4^m} \binom{2m}{m} \right]^3 = \frac{\Gamma(1/4)^4}{4\pi^3} \tag{6.8} \]

This is Watson’s integral \(I_1\) (Watson, 1939). The connection to \(\Gamma(1/4)\) arises because the central binomial coefficients \(\binom{2m}{m}/4^m \to 1/\sqrt{\pi m}\) asymptotically, and their cube generates a hypergeometric function \({}_3F_2\) that reduces to products of Gamma values at quarter-integer arguments.

8.5 Theorem 6.1 (Watson–\(G^*\) identity)

\[ G_{\text{BCC}}(0) = \frac{\Gamma(1/4)^4}{4\pi^3} = \frac{G^{*2}}{2\pi} \tag{6.9} \]

where \(G^* = \Gamma(1/4)^2 / (\sqrt{2}\pi)\) is the lemniscatic bridge constant defined in Chapter 9.

Proof. The first equality is Watson (1939), proved by contour integration or by the Chowla-Selberg formula applied to the CM curve \(E_i: y^2 = x^3 - x\). The second equality is algebraic: \[ \frac{G^{*2}}{2\pi} = \frac{[\Gamma(1/4)^2/(\sqrt{2}\pi)]^2}{2\pi} = \frac{\Gamma(1/4)^4}{2\pi^2 \cdot 2\pi} = \frac{\Gamma(1/4)^4}{4\pi^3} \quad \square \]

8.6 6.5 Why SC and FCC cannot produce this identity [THEOREM]

The SC Laplacian eigenvalue \(\sigma_{\text{SC}} = 1 - (\cos k_1 + \cos k_2 + \cos k_3)/3\) is a sum, not a product. The Green’s function \(1/\sigma_{\text{SC}}\) does not admit a geometric series that factors across axes. The SC Watson integral evaluates to a different number (\(\approx 1.516\) in the normalized convention used here), involving a more complex combination of Gamma values. It does not equal \(G^{*2}/(2\pi)\).

Similarly, the FCC eigenvalue involves pairwise products \(\cos k_\mu \cos k_\nu\), which partially factor but not completely. The FCC Watson integral is a third distinct number.

Numerical confirmation (scripts/exploration/gap_equation_layer_convergence.py): On periodic tori of size \(L = 64, 96, 128\), the BCC Green’s function converges to \(1.3932 = G^{*2}/(2\pi)\) while the SC Green’s function converges to \(\approx 1.516\) (a different value):

\(L\) \(G_{\text{BCC}}(0)\) \(G_{\text{SC}}(0)\) Target: \(G^{*2}/(2\pi)\)
64 1.3650 1.4952 1.3932
96 1.3744 1.5023 1.3932
128 1.3791 1.5058 1.3932

BCC converges to the target; SC does not. The BCC’s slower convergence (1.0% remaining at \(L = 128\) vs. SC’s 8% overshoot) is due to its 4 zero modes causing stronger finite-size effects.

8.7 6.6 Physical interpretation

The Watson–\(G^*\) identity connects lattice geometry (the BCC Green’s function) to number theory (the Gamma function at quarter-integers) to algebraic geometry (the CM elliptic curve \(E_i\)). In subsequent chapters:

  • Chapter 7: \(G^*\) is identified as the bridge constant connecting the lattice to the lemniscate.
  • Chapter 8: The gap equation uses \(16 G^{*2}\) as its coefficient, producing the master quadratic.
  • Chapter 18: The same BCC multiplicative structure that produces \(G^*\) also generates the \(SU(3)\) gauge group — these are not independent facts but two aspects of the triple cosine product.

The BCC sublattice is the structural heart of the theory. Its multiplicative eigenvalue is the single algebraic fact from which both the coupling constant and the gauge group flow.
