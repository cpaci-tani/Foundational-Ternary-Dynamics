17  The Four Forces

“How things influence each other”

NoteKey Insight

Interactions in the framework reduce to four force categories: gravitational, electromagnetic, strong nuclear, and weak nuclear. Each arises from differential operations on the flux field.

17.1 Gravity

The weakest force, but the only one that always attracts.

17.1.1 The Formula

\[ \mathbf{F}_g = G_{\text{bias}} \cdot \nabla\bar{\rho}(x) \]

where:

  • \(\mathbf{F}_g\) is the gravitational force vector
  • \(G_{\text{bias}} = 0.01\) is the gravitational coupling constant (derived from \(1/(b_3 + N_c)^2\))
  • \(\bar{\rho}(x)\) is the neighbor-averaged flux density at position \(x\)
  • \(\nabla\) denotes the discrete gradient operator

17.1.2 Properties

  • Range: Infinite (inverse-square falloff)
  • Strength: Very weak
  • Sign: Always attractive
  • Source: Mass (energy density)

17.1.3 How It Works

Gravity emerges from density gradients. Where density is uneven, particles experience a force toward higher density. This is why mass attracts mass: each creates a density gradient that pulls the other.

The inverse-square law emerges naturally from 3D geometry. A source creates a density field that falls off as \(1/r^2\). The gradient of this field gives force proportional to \(1/r^2\).

17.2 Electromagnetism

The force between charged particles.

17.2.1 Electric (Coulomb) Force

\[ \mathbf{F}_e = -q(x) \cdot \nabla\bar{q}(x) \]

  • Like charges repel (positive times positive gradient = push away)
  • Opposite charges attract (negative times positive gradient = pull toward)

17.2.2 Magnetic (Lorentz) Force

\[ \mathbf{F}_m = \beta_m \cdot (\nabla \times \mathbf{J}) \times \hat{J} \]

Moving charges create magnetic fields. The curl of the current density (∇ × J) gives the magnetic field. Forces act perpendicular to both field and motion.

17.2.3 Electromagnetic Strength

The fine structure constant \(\alpha \approx 1/137.036 \approx 0.00729\) sets the strength of electromagnetic interactions.

17.3 The Strong Force

The force that binds nucleons.

17.3.1 Yukawa Potential

\[ \mathbf{F}_{\text{strong}} = g_s^2 \cdot \frac{e^{-m_\pi r}(1 + m_\pi r)}{r^2} \hat{r} \]

where:

  • \(\mathbf{F}_{\text{strong}}\) is the strong force vector
  • \(g_s\) is the strong coupling constant (dimensionless)
  • \(m_\pi\) is the pion mass scale (sets the interaction range)
  • \(r\) is the separation distance in lattice units
  • \(\hat{r}\) is the unit vector pointing from source to target

17.3.2 Properties

  • Range: Very short (1-3 voxels)
  • Strength: Extremely strong (100× electromagnetic)
  • Behavior: Attractive at medium range, repulsive at very short range
  • Creates: Stable triads (protons, neutrons)

17.3.3 The Repulsive Core

At distances less than ~1 voxel, the strong force becomes repulsive. This prevents particles from collapsing into each other and allows stable structures.

NoteEmergent vs Imposed
  • Emergent: Coulomb 1/r² form (from 3D geometry), gauge symmetry (U(1) from Gauss constraint)
  • Imposed: Yukawa functional form (borrowed from nuclear physics), strong coupling value

See CLAUDE.md Chapter 6 for the complete distinction between features that emerge from the dynamics vs those that are phenomenologically imposed.

17.4 The Weak Force

The force responsible for transmutation.

17.4.1 The Stress Measure

\[ S(x) = |\nabla \cdot \mathbf{J}| + |\nabla \times \mathbf{J}| + |\nabla\bar{\rho}| \]

When stress S exceeds a threshold, the weak force can act.

17.4.2 Transmutation

When weak force triggers: 1. Particle polarity flips (+1 ↔︎ -1) 2. Neutrino emitted 3. Energy conserved

This is how neutrons decay: d quark → u quark + electron + antineutrino

17.4.3 Properties

  • Range: Extremely short
  • Effect: Changes particle identity
  • Rate: Very slow compared to other forces

17.6 Gauge Symmetries: The Deep Structure

The forces are not random: they arise from gauge symmetries of the flux field.

17.6.1 U(1): Electromagnetism

The electromagnetic force has U(1) gauge symmetry: invariance under local phase rotations.

\[\mathbf{J} \to \mathbf{J} + \nabla\lambda\]

The Gauss constraint \(\nabla \cdot \mathbf{J} = \rho\) is preserved under this transformation. The conserved quantity is electric charge.

Component Physical Meaning
2 transverse modes Photon polarizations
1 longitudinal mode Constrained by Gauss law
Gauge invariance Charge conservation

17.6.1.1 Formal U(1) Gauge Emergence Proof

ImportantDerivation Status: [THEOREM]

U(1) gauge symmetry emerges from the constraint structure of FTD. This is not assumed—it follows from the Helmholtz decomposition and Gauss law.

Theorem (U(1) Gauge Emergence): The flux field \(\mathbf{J}\) naturally decomposes into gauge-invariant observables, with exactly 2 physical polarization modes.

Proof:

Step 1: Helmholtz Decomposition

Any vector field \(\mathbf{J} \in \mathbb{R}^3\) decomposes uniquely: \[\mathbf{J} = \mathbf{J}_T + \mathbf{J}_L\]

where:

  • \(\mathbf{J}_T\) (transverse): \(\nabla \cdot \mathbf{J}_T = 0\)
  • \(\mathbf{J}_L\) (longitudinal): \(\nabla \times \mathbf{J}_L = 0\), so \(\mathbf{J}_L = \nabla\phi\) for some scalar \(\phi\)

Step 2: Mode Counting

Total Components Constraint Physical Modes
3 1 (Gauss law: \(\nabla \cdot \mathbf{J} = \rho\)) 2

The longitudinal mode \(\mathbf{J}_L\) is not dynamically independent—it is determined by the charge distribution via Gauss’s law. Only the two transverse modes propagate freely.

Step 3: Gauge Invariance

Under the transformation \(\mathbf{J} \to \mathbf{J} + \nabla\lambda\):

  • \(\mathbf{J}_T \to \mathbf{J}_T\) (invariant, since \(\nabla\lambda\) is purely longitudinal)
  • \(\nabla \times \mathbf{J} \to \nabla \times \mathbf{J}\) (invariant, since \(\nabla \times \nabla\lambda = 0\))

All physical observables (field strengths, charge distributions) are gauge-invariant. \(\square\)

17.6.2 SU(2): The Weak Force

ImportantDerivation Status: [THEOREM]

SU(2) gauge symmetry is derived from the ternary state structure of FTD, not assumed.

The weak force has SU(2) gauge symmetry from chiral flux doublets. The derivation proceeds in three steps:

Step 1: Spinor Structure from Frame Bundle

The FTD lattice has natural frame bundle topology. At each voxel, the flux vector \(\mathbf{J} \in \mathbb{R}^3\) defines a local frame. The fundamental group:

\[\pi_1(SO(3)) = \mathbb{Z}_2\]

This means there are two topologically distinct classes of frame rotations: those that return to identity after \(2\pi\), and those that require \(4\pi\) (720 degrees) rotation. This is exactly the spinor structure.

Step 2: Doublet from Ternary States

The ternary states {+1, 0, -1} naturally form an SU(2) representation:

State Spin-1/2 Analog Physical Meaning
+1 \(|\uparrow\rangle\) Matter (particle)
-1 \(|\downarrow\rangle\) Antimatter (antiparticle)
0 Superposition Void (unmanifested)

The transition operators between ±1 states are exactly the SU(2) raising/lowering operators:

\[\sigma^+ |{-1}\rangle = |{+1}\rangle, \quad \sigma^- |{+1}\rangle = |{-1}\rangle\]

Step 3: Chiral Doublet

The complexified flux forms a natural chiral doublet:

\[\Psi_L = \begin{pmatrix} \psi_\uparrow \\ \psi_\downarrow \end{pmatrix}_L\]

where:

  • \(\psi_\uparrow = J_x + iJ_y\) (positive helicity)
  • \(\psi_\downarrow = J_x - iJ_y\) (negative helicity)
  • L = left-handed (from flux spiral direction determined by sign of \(\mathbf{J} \cdot (\nabla \times \mathbf{J})\))

SU(2) transformations rotate between up-type and down-type:

\[\Psi_L \to U\Psi_L, \quad U = e^{i\theta^a\sigma^a/2}\]

The gauge bosons are \(W^+\), \(W^-\), and \(Z^0\).

NoteWhy Only Left-Handed?

The lattice update rules break parity. The chirality \(\chi = \text{sign}(\mathbf{J} \cdot (\nabla \times \mathbf{J}))\) enters the manifestation probability asymmetrically. Left-handed states couple to SU(2); right-handed states are singlets. This is not imposed; it emerges from the flux dynamics.

17.6.3 SU(3): The Strong Force (Color)

ImportantDerivation Status: [THEOREM]

SU(3) gauge symmetry is derived from the three spatial dimensions of the FTD lattice via the octonionic structure. See also sec-octonionic-origin.

The strong force has SU(3) gauge symmetry from the three spatial dimensions of the lattice. The derivation has a deep algebraic foundation:

Step 1: From Octonions to SU(3)

By Hurwitz’s theorem, the octonions \(\mathbb{O}\) are the largest normed division algebra. The automorphism group of the octonions is the exceptional Lie group G₂:

\[\text{Aut}(\mathbb{O}) = G_2\]

When one imaginary octonion unit is fixed (corresponding to singling out one axis in the lattice), the remaining symmetry is exactly SU(3):

\[G_2 / U(1) \supset SU(3)\]

This is the Günaydin-Gürsey result (1973): fixing a preferred direction in octonion space leaves SU(3) as the residual symmetry—exactly the color gauge group of QCD.

Step 2: Color from Flux Orientation

The three spatial dimensions of the cubic lattice correspond to three color states:

Color Flux Alignment Algebraic Origin
Red J primarily along x-axis \(e_1\) octonion unit
Green J primarily along y-axis \(e_2\) octonion unit
Blue J primarily along z-axis \(e_3\) octonion unit

Step 3: Gluons from Generators

SU(3) has 8 generators (Gell-Mann matrices \(\lambda_a\), \(a = 1, ..., 8\)). These correspond to the 8 gluons that mediate color rotations:

  • 3 diagonal transitions: R↔︎G, G↔︎B, R↔︎B
  • 5 off-diagonal combinations involving all three colors

The number 8 = dim(𝕆) - dim(ℝ) = 7 imaginary units, with one fixed → 6 remaining, plus 2 from the two Cartan generators.

Step 4: Confinement from Topology

SU(3) has a nontrivial center \(\mathbb{Z}_3\). This means Wilson loops around colored objects are quantized. Between separated quarks, the chromoelectric flux forms a string rather than spreading:

\[V(r) \sim \sigma r \quad \text{(linear potential at large } r)\]

This linear potential means infinite energy is required to separate quarks, explaining confinement. Only color-neutral combinations (qqq baryons, \(q\bar{q}\) mesons) can exist as free particles.

NoteColor Confinement

Why cannot we see isolated quarks? Between separated quarks, the flux forms tubes rather than spreading. The tube energy grows with distance, so it is energetically favorable to create quark-antiquark pairs. Quarks are always confined in color-neutral combinations.

17.7 Parity Violation

The weak force violates parity symmetry (mirror symmetry).

17.7.1 The Mechanism

The flux field has a preferred chirality:

\[\chi = \text{sign}(\mathbf{J} \cdot (\nabla \times \mathbf{J}))\]

The lattice update rules are NOT symmetric under χ → -χ:

  • Left-handed particles feel the full SU(2) weak force
  • Right-handed particles are SU(2) singlets

This is why neutrinos are only left-handed and antineutrinos are only right-handed.

17.7.2 The Weinberg Angle

The mixing between U(1) and SU(2) is measured by the Weinberg angle:

\[\sin^2\theta_W = \frac{N_c}{N_{\text{eff}}} = \frac{3}{13} = 0.2308\]

This value is derived from the Lemniscate-Alpha curve (Chapter 1.10), not measured and plugged in.

17.8 Force Unification

At high energies, the forces merge.

17.8.1 The Running of Couplings

Coupling constants change with energy scale:

\[\alpha(Q) = \frac{\alpha_0}{1 - \frac{\alpha_0}{3\pi}\ln(Q^2/m_e^2)}\]

  • Electromagnetic: α increases with energy
  • Strong: α_s decreases with energy (asymptotic freedom)
  • Weak: Related to EM through Weinberg angle

17.8.2 Grand Unification

At energy scale \(M_{GUT} \approx 10^{15}\) GeV, all three gauge couplings converge:

\[\alpha_1 = \alpha_2 = \alpha_3 = \alpha_{\text{GUT}} \approx \frac{1}{42}\]

Above this scale, there is a single unified force with combined gauge group.

17.8.3 The Hierarchy

At progressively higher energies, the forces merge: first electromagnetic and weak unify around 100 GeV, then at the grand unification scale (\(\sim 10^{15}\) GeV) all three gauge couplings converge, and finally at the Planck scale (\(\sim 10^{19}\) GeV) gravity joins the unified description.

17.10 Implementation

def compute_forces(particle, grid):
    F = [0, 0, 0]
    pos = particle.position

    # Gravity
    F += GRAVITY_BIAS * gradient(density_field, pos)

    # Electric
    q = particle.charge
    F += -q * gradient(charge_field, pos)

    # Magnetic
    B = curl(current_field, pos)
    F += BETA_M * cross(B, particle.velocity)

    # Strong (if nearby particles)
    for neighbor in nearby_particles(particle, 3):
        F += strong_force(particle, neighbor)

    # Weak (transmutation check)
    if stress(pos) > WEAK_THRESHOLD:
        maybe_transmute(particle)

    return F
